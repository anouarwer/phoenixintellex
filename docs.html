01

The Problem With Static Scalars

A single expected-recovery scalar — the canonical output of a discounted cash flow model applied to a non-performing loan — is epistemically insufficient as a pricing basis for two compounding reasons. First, it collapses a distribution of possible outcomes into a point estimate without communicating the shape, skew, or tail weight of that distribution; a portfolio priced at a 38% expected recovery rate may carry a 95th-percentile recovery of 61% or 19% depending on the correlation structure of the underlying collateral, and a buyer who cannot distinguish between these cases cannot rationally bid. Second, static scalars are conditionally biased: they are calibrated on historical recovery data that encodes the macroeconomic conditions prevailing at calibration time, and they degrade precisely when accurate pricing is most necessary — during periods of macroeconomic stress that depart from the calibration regime.

The Mathematical Framework

The engine operates across three mathematical layers, each with a defined assumption space. The framework is stated below without implementation detail; the reader is invited to verify or contest the formulation on mathematical grounds.

EQ. 1 Aᵢ = √ρ ⋅ M + √(1−ρ) ⋅ Zᵢ
P(defaultᵢ | M) = Φ( (Φ⁻¹(PDᵢ) − √ρ ⋅ M) / √(1−ρ) )
Aᵢ — latent creditworthiness variable for obligor i
M — systematic factor; M ∼ N(0,1)
ρ — asset correlation parameter; constrained to (0,1)
03

The Correlation Problem

The parameter ρ in Eq. 1 is not the observed pairwise recovery correlation R between loans in the portfolio. It is the asset correlation — the fraction of variance in a loan's latent creditworthiness attributable to the shared systematic factor M. The distinction is not pedantic. Basel III IRB framework specifies asset correlation as a decreasing function of PD for performing corporate exposures, ranging from 12% to 24%. Applying these figures to an NPL portfolio is a category error.
