The Monte Carlo Advantage in European NPL Pricing

BAYESIAN-QUANT FRAMEWORK // DISTRESSED CREDIT // EU-27 JURISDICTIONS

01

The Problem With Static Scalars

A single expected-recovery scalar — the canonical output of a discounted cash flow model applied to a non-performing loan — is epistemically insufficient as a pricing basis for two compounding reasons. First, it collapses a distribution of possible outcomes into a point estimate without communicating the shape, skew, or tail weight of that distribution; a portfolio priced at a 38% expected recovery rate may carry a 95th-percentile recovery of 61% or 19% depending on the correlation structure of the underlying collateral, and a buyer who cannot distinguish between these cases cannot rationally bid. Second, static scalars are conditionally biased: they are calibrated on historical recovery data that encodes the macroeconomic conditions prevailing at calibration time, and they degrade precisely when accurate pricing is most necessary — during periods of macroeconomic stress that depart from the calibration regime. The Iberian NPL cycle of 2008–2016 provides the most thoroughly documented empirical case for this failure. Spanish and Portuguese banks, operating under EBA stress-test frameworks that applied performing-loan loss-given-default assumptions to distressed portfolios, systematically under-reserved against recovery shortfalls on mortgage-backed NPL books. The collateral co-movement that materialized — driven by regional unemployment shocks and simultaneous judicial system saturation across both jurisdictions — was structurally absent from single-scenario static models. The consequence was not a model error in the narrow sense; it was an architectural error. The model class was wrong for the problem.

The Mathematical Framework

The engine operates across three mathematical layers, each with a defined assumption space. The framework is stated below without implementation detail; the reader is invited to verify or contest the formulation on mathematical grounds.

EQ. 1 Aᵢ = √ρ ⋅ M + √(1−ρ) ⋅ Zᵢ P(defaultᵢ | M) = Φ( (Φ⁻¹(PDᵢ) − √ρ ⋅ M) / √(1−ρ) )
Aᵢ — latent creditworthiness variable for obligor i M — systematic factor; M ∼ N(0,1), drawn independently per Monte Carlo path Zᵢ — idiosyncratic shock; Zᵢ ∼ N(0,1), drawn independently per obligor per path ρ — asset correlation parameter; constrained to (0,1); calibrated on distressed cohort data, not Basel III performing-loan tables Φ — standard normal CDF; PDᵢ is the unconditional probability of default for obligor i
EQ. 2 P(θ | D) ∝ P(D | θ) ⋅ P(θ) μₚ = (σₚ² ⋅ μ₀ + σ₀² ⋅ x̅) / (σₚ² + σ₀²) σₚ² = (σ₀² ⋅ σₗ²/n) / (σ₀² + σₗ²/n)
θ — recovery rate parameter vector for the portfolio segment D — observed recovery data from closed cohorts in the same legal jurisdiction μ₀, σ₀² — prior mean and variance; sourced from EBA NPL transaction database and servicer workout statistics x̅, σₗ², n — sample mean, variance, and count of observed closed-loan recoveries in the upload cohort μₚ, σₚ² — posterior mean and variance; replaces the flat prior in all downstream Monte Carlo paths Assumption: conjugate normal-normal model; likelihood variance σₗ² estimated from intra-cohort dispersion.
EQ. 3 NPV(r) = −P₀ + ∑ₜ₌¹ᵀ [ CFₜ / (1 + r)ₜ ] = 0 r* = Brent(f := NPV, [rₘᴵₙ, rₘₐˣ], ε = 10⁻⁶)
P₀ — portfolio acquisition price; the unknown variable in bid-price inversion CFₜ — expected cash flow at period t; drawn from the Monte Carlo recovery distribution for each path T — maximum recovery horizon; set at 120 months for unsecured consumer NPL; 240 months for secured mortgage NPL r* — internal rate of return; solved per Monte Carlo path; the output distribution of r* across N=10,000 paths constitutes the IRR distribution ε — convergence tolerance; Brent's method guarantees superlinear convergence; no Newton initialization required Assumption: cash flows are deterministic within each Monte Carlo path conditional on the drawn systematic factor M. Reinvestment rate is not assumed.
03

The Correlation Problem in Distressed Portfolios

The parameter ρ in Eq. 1 is not the observed pairwise recovery correlation R between loans in the portfolio. It is the asset correlation — the fraction of variance in a loan's latent creditworthiness attributable to the shared systematic factor M. The distinction is not pedantic. Basel III IRB framework specifies asset correlation as a decreasing function of PD for performing corporate exposures, ranging from 12% to 24%. Applying these figures to an NPL portfolio is a category error for three reasons.

First, non-performing loans have by definition already defaulted; the PD is not the forward probability of a performing exposure crossing a threshold, but a certainty already realized. The Basel formula's conditioning on forward-looking PD is therefore inapplicable. Second, recovery on an NPL is driven overwhelmingly by collateral liquidation speed and judicial process duration, not by the borrower's ongoing creditworthiness. Both of these factors are highly co-dependent within a jurisdiction: a systemic shock that saturates the court system simultaneously impairs recovery for all secured NPLs in that jurisdiction, regardless of original loan characteristics. Third, the observed recovery correlation R in Iberian NPL cohorts during 2012–2016 ranged from 0.28 to 0.47 for mortgage-backed exposures, well above the Basel III ceiling. A model using ρ = 0.15 will under-price systemic risk by a factor that depends nonlinearly on the portfolio size and the depth of the stress scenario.

The calibration methodology for ρ uses jurisdiction-stratified empirical priors with portfolio-level refinement where observation count permits. The ρ estimate and its 90% credible interval are reported as a primary output of every pricing run.
